Why this optimization works

I is stiffness geometry, not the applied moment M. For the uniform cantilever, Mmax = F L/2 and δ = F L³/(8 E I). Raising I reduces displacement. Changing a, b or τ does not change M for this fixed external load and span.

At constant total F, a longer beam has greater mass and greater bending and shear droop. Therefore the best allowed span is L = 12 m. For each trial height and thickness, I is linear in flange width: I = b[a³ − (a−2τ)³]/12 + τ(a−2τ)³/12. We solve for the smallest b that meets the selected droop target (and b ≥ τ), then either test every height/thickness pair on a finite grid or descend in those two scaled variables. Grid search rounds width upward to the next allowed grid value and verifies strict feasibility; this is equivalent to a full three-dimensional grid for finding minimum mass, because all wider feasible sections for the same height/thickness weigh more.

Finite-difference gradients, projected bounds, and backtracking line search reduce mass. Ten starts, including your current geometry, help with local minima. The assignment solver sizes to the Euler–Bernoulli equations from the brief. The optional Timoshenko beam model lives on its own tab and is not included in the assignment graph.

Height often buys the most I per added kilogram because it moves both flanges farther from the neutral axis. But “a is cubed” is not the whole argument: the hollow-section subtraction also changes. The relevant comparison is (∂I/∂x)/(∂m/∂x), holding other dimensions fixed. These local marginal efficiencies depend on the current geometry, and are not a global optimization proof. Once height reaches 1000 mm, further increases are forbidden by C4.
